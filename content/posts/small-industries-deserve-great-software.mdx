The size of an industry shouldn’t determine the quality of its software.

People working in specialized fields deserve thoughtful design, broad capabilities, frequent improvements, and products that understand how they actually work. A smaller market doesn’t make those needs less important. It has just made the economics of meeting them harder.

That’s what makes this moment in software so exciting.

Since 2020, I’ve spent a lot of time studying companies like Linear and Stripe. Their attention to detail is part of the appeal, but so is the experience of being their customer: getting responsive help in Linear’s Slack community or talking through a complex problem with Stripe’s support team in Discord. Those experiences build trust. They’ve certainly made me an advocate.

The ambition is to bring that same level of care to software built for much smaller industries.

## Deeply specialized, without a lower quality bar

Building for a niche has often meant difficult tradeoffs. There are fewer customers to support the cost of a team, yet the work can be just as complex. Customers still need reliability, integrations, thoughtful workflows, and the small details that make a product feel good to use.

I don’t think SaaS is dead. I think we’re entering a period where more of those tradeoffs can be challenged.

AI is changing what focused teams can build and maintain. The opportunity goes well beyond launching a narrow tool quickly. It becomes more practical to build deeply specialized software with the breadth to support more of an industry’s work, a high quality bar, and a steady pace of meaningful improvement.

Residential technology integration is one example. The CEDIA channel has its own workflows, terminology, and long-term client relationships. Software built around those realities can go further than asking people to adapt their businesses to a general-purpose tool.

## Feedback has to become action

Better tools don’t automatically produce better products. The advantage belongs to teams that understand their customers and put that understanding to work.

Listening matters, but so does responding. Taking a request seriously, understanding the need behind it, building a useful solution, and returning to refine it. Customers should be able to recognize their feedback in the product they use.

Shipping often matters when it delivers that kind of progress. Quality shows up in the reliability of a workflow, the clarity of an interaction, and the care taken with something that might look minor on a feature list but affects someone every day.

## Beyond a single interface

The way we use software is opening up, too. A well-designed web app can be one way into a product, rather than the only way.

That’s part of the motivation for doubling down on agentic processes and headless software: capabilities that can be used through APIs, integrations, or agents as well as an interface. Someone might work directly in the app, ask an agent to complete a task, or access the same capabilities through another system.

In that world, a product’s understanding of an industry matters even more. The workflows, rules, and context still need to be right, whichever way the software is used.

This is what motivates my work at OneVision, alongside a team that cares deeply about quality and the people we build for. But the opportunity is much broader than any one company or industry.

Small markets can support ambitious software. Teams can cover more ground without losing their focus on the details. Customers can have products built around their work, with people behind them who respond and keep making things better.

That’s a future worth building toward: great software in places that have been hard to serve, earning trust and becoming something customers genuinely love.
